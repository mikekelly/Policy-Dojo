## Executive Summary

**Goal:** Make housing affordable by allowing supply to meet demand.

**Thesis:** UK housing is expensive because government restricts supply. Planning permission, zoning rules, and building regulations make it illegal to build enough homes where people want to live. When supply cannot respond to demand, prices rise. The solution is to remove these barriers and let people build.

**Key Proposals:**
1. Replace discretionary planning permission with zoning by-right
2. Remove height restrictions and density limits in urban areas
3. Abolish green belt protections within 10 miles of major cities
4. End section 106 requirements and community infrastructure levy
5. Eliminate parking minimums

## The Problem

UK house prices rose 400% from 1995 to 2020 while average incomes rose 150%. The average UK home now costs 9x average earnings. In London it's 13x. Young people cannot afford homes. Families live in cramped conditions. Workers cannot move to where jobs are.

This is not a market failure. It is a regulatory failure. We have made it illegal to build enough housing.

## Root Cause Analysis

**Planning permission creates artificial scarcity.** Each new home requires permission from local councils. Councils face incentives to reject applications—existing residents oppose new building ("not in my backyard"), but future residents cannot vote or attend hearings. The visible cost is a building site next door; the invisible cost is millions priced out of housing.

**Zoning prevents density.** Most of London and other cities are frozen at low density by rules written decades ago. We ban apartments in areas zoned for single-family homes. We ban taller buildings in areas that could support them. This restricts supply precisely where demand is highest.

**The green belt forces sprawl.** 13% of England is designated green belt—not nature reserves or parks, but land where building is banned. Much of it is ordinary farmland or scrubland ringing cities. This forces development far from jobs and amenities, increases commute times, and still doesn't prevent development—it just moves it farther out.

**Developer taxes make homes more expensive.** Section 106 agreements and the Community Infrastructure Levy require developers to fund local services, roads, schools. These costs get passed to buyers. They also give councils veto power—negotiate for more "contributions" or reject the application.

**Parking minimums waste land.** Rules requiring parking spaces per unit force developments to dedicate land to cars rather than homes. This reduces density and increases costs.

## Proposed Solution

### 1. Zoning By-Right

Replace discretionary planning permission with clear zoning rules. If your proposal meets the zoning criteria, you can build—no council approval needed.

Zoning criteria specify: maximum height, setbacks, land use. If you comply, proceed. No neighbor objections, no council hearings, no months of uncertainty.

### 2. Remove Density Restrictions

Eliminate height limits and density maximums in urban areas. Let builders respond to demand. If people want apartments, let builders construct apartments. If land is expensive, let builders build upward.

### 3. Abolish Inner Green Belt

Remove green belt designation within 10 miles of major city centers. Owners can sell or develop their land without permission. Actual parks and nature reserves remain protected through specific designations.

### 4. End Developer Taxes

Eliminate section 106 and CIL. Fund infrastructure through general taxation. This removes council leverage over developers and reduces per-unit costs.

### 5. Eliminate Parking Minimums

Let developers and buyers decide parking needs. Dense urban areas need less parking. New residents without cars shouldn't pay for parking they won't use.

## Risk Analysis

### Economic Transition Risks

**Risk:** Rapid policy change causes short-term disruption.

**Mitigation:** Changes apply to new applications immediately but don't invalidate existing permissions or under-construction projects.

**Risk:** Existing homeowners see property values decline.

**Reality Check:** This is a feature, not a bug. Housing affordability requires prices to stop rising faster than incomes. Current owners receive a windfall from artificial scarcity—it's unfair to demand future generations remain priced out to protect that windfall. Note: locations with genuine amenity value and supply constraints will maintain value; much current pricing reflects scarcity, not amenity.

### Quality and Safety Risks

**Risk:** Removing regulations leads to poor quality or unsafe housing.

**Counter:** Building safety regulations remain—structural standards, fire safety, sanitation. We remove restrictions on quantity and location, not quality.

**Risk:** Developers build cheaply and pocket savings.

**Counter:** In competitive markets, buyers choose quality. With more builders competing, quality improves. Current planning process actually reduces quality—long approval processes favor large developers with capital to wait out decisions.

### Local Infrastructure Strain

**Risk:** New housing overwhelms schools, roads, utilities.

**Reality:** Current system doesn't solve this—it just concentrates infrastructure strain in areas that get permission while depriving other areas of investment. More homes mean more council tax revenue. Infrastructure should be funded through taxation, not ransoming planning permission.

**Risk:** Areas of high demand will see rapid building.

**Counter:** That's the point. If an area has high demand, it needs more housing. Price signals work—when areas fill up and congestion rises, development naturally disperses.

### Environmental and Community Character

**Risk:** Loss of green spaces and community character.

**Response:** Most "green belt" isn't accessible green space—it's private farmland. Genuine parks remain protected. "Community character" often means "keep others out." Every current resident was once a new arrival. Character evolves; neighborhoods change. The real character of Britain includes welcoming new residents and allowing communities to grow.

**Risk:** Urban sprawl and car dependency.

**Counter:** Current policy forces sprawl by banning urban density. This policy enables dense, walkable development. Removing parking minimums further reduces car dependency.

## Real-World Evidence

**Japan:** Tokyo demonstrates this approach works. Japan uses by-right zoning nationally. Property owners can build without neighbor permission if they comply with zoning. Result: Tokyo housing prices stayed roughly flat for 20 years despite Tokyo growing by millions of people. London's population grew similarly, but London prices tripled.

Confounding factors: Japan has declining national population, different cultural preferences for housing. However, Tokyo itself grew significantly, and the price stability occurred in a growing city—this is strong evidence that supply responsiveness matters.

**New Zealand (recent reform):** In 2020, New Zealand eliminated single-family zoning in most urban areas, allowing townhouses and small apartments by-right. Early data shows building consents increasing sharply. Too soon for price effects, but supply response is rapid.

**Auckland (prior to reform):** Pre-reform Auckland had restrictive planning similar to UK. Housing prices rose dramatically. Post-reform data will be informative.

**Houston, Texas:** Minimal zoning, no planning permission for most development. Houston remains affordable despite strong growth. However, confounding factors include abundant flat land, car-centric development, and different cultural expectations. Still demonstrates that lack of supply restrictions prevents price spirals.

**Disconfirming evidence - San Francisco:** Liberalized some approval processes but kept strict height limits, environmental review requirements, and neighborhood veto points. Prices remain extremely high. This suggests partial reform is insufficient—must address multiple barriers simultaneously.

**Cambridge, UK:** Deliberate tight restrictions on building to "preserve character." Result: housing costs exclude researchers and university staff, requiring longer commutes. Shows that restricting supply doesn't preserve communities—it just changes who can afford to live there.

## Implementation Timeline

**Immediate (Year 1):**
- Legislation passed establishing by-right zoning framework
- Councils required to publish clear zoning maps
- Section 106 and CIL eliminated
- Parking minimums removed

**Near-term (Years 2-3):**
- Density restrictions removed in urban areas
- Green belt eliminated within 10 miles of major cities
- First wave of new construction begins

**Medium-term (Years 4-10):**
- Housing supply increases significantly
- Price growth moderates, then stabilizes
- Increased housing enables labor mobility and economic growth

## Expected Outcomes

**Housing affordability:** Prices stabilize relative to incomes as supply responds to demand. New construction focuses on high-demand areas. More people can afford homes near work.

**Economic mobility:** Workers can move to high-productivity cities without housing costs consuming all wage gains. Young people can form households and start families earlier.

**Innovation in housing:** More builders enter market with new approaches—modular construction, different housing types, micro-apartments, co-housing. Competition drives innovation.

**Urban vitality:** Denser neighborhoods support more local businesses, better transit, walkable amenities.

**Reduced inequality:** Current system concentrates gains among existing property owners while excluding renters and future buyers. Reform distributes housing more broadly.

## Conclusion

Housing unaffordability is a policy choice. We chose to make building illegal, and predictably, prices rose. The solution is not to subsidize demand through help-to-buy schemes or tax landlords—these treat symptoms while making the disease worse.

The solution is simple: let people build. When supply can respond to demand, markets clear at affordable prices. The challenge is political—existing homeowners benefit from scarcity and resist reform. But continuing current policy condemns future generations to poverty or exile from cities where opportunities exist.

The evidence is clear. Cities that allow building stay affordable. Cities that restrict building become expensive. We must choose: protect incumbent homeowners' windfall gains, or enable millions to access housing. The choice is obvious. Let people build.
